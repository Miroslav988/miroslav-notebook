Not in the CV

It started in a restaurant. I was packing delivery orders and decided something had to change. A family friend built websites, so I asked if I could try; he took my learning on himself, and his was the first code I copied. Economics at Novosibirsk State stayed unfinished. Code became the thing I'd do without being asked.

Bangkok was too quiet. I have my own flat there and it's calm, calmer than I need. Amsterdam suits me better: an English-speaking way of thinking has always felt closer to mine, something I first noticed as a schoolboy, during a year at a school in Oxford.

How the startup happened. At a conference I was doing what I always do, sitting in a corner with a laptop, working. Someone came over and asked what I was building. That conversation became Renairo: a product for analysing and building sales teams. The hardest part of year one was not the code, it was figuring out what to build at all. I burned through a lot of theories before one worked.

Flame stays private. I built it for myself and I use it. I'm not releasing it to other people: it holds personal data, and doing that properly means studying the privacy law in depth, which I don't have the time to do honestly. So it stays mine.

The mistake I keep making. Forgetting to watch leftover build artifacts on VM disks. The disk fills up, I lose access, and everything gets reset. It has happened more than once. It's on the list.

My desk. Two laptops: a Mac running macOS and Linux, and a Windows one. A monitor, coffee, a book, and a paper notebook. This page is modelled on that notebook.

What I can't stand is routine. I want to build something new, always. The flip side: outside work I mostly work. When I don't, it's active rest. And the bench press is real: 125 kg.

English. Learned everywhere, all the time. Working in it daily is the point.

How I work

On security. I keep my own cybersecurity wiki and use it when I build auth, access control and deployments. I constantly form theories about how the app could be broken, then test them. "What can go wrong when this runs" is part of the design.

On AI tools. Use them all day, review everything. They like to leak server env vars to the client with a public prefix, or open all ports both ways "for debugging".

On the memory leak. One container leaked slowly for days until it got restarted under load. Now memory limits, metrics and alerts go in on day one. Observability is part of the feature.

On disagreements. A lead once asked me to fix "my" bad code. Git blame said it was his. I fixed it and pushed. The product didn't care whose it was.
